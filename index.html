    Evidence for repair

    • Documented practice, general Columns are repaired or replaced locally by jacking up the structure, with the roof left in place: 瑞巌寺本堂, 三重県庁舎, 本山寺霊廟. None of these cases is at a daimochi.
    • Geometry, this model The list above is derived from the contact limits between parts: with a post below and dowels, the post comes out with nothing else taken apart.
    • Historical text and test The joint is held closed by load from above (Saitō 1904, via Shimoyama). In Wang's tension tests, the clamped joint pulled back into position (Wang 2024).
    • Not claimed here Whether a given frame stays sound while the support is out depends on its loads and members.

      Record history and use

      Dai means platform and mochi to hold up. The joint splices two beams directly above a column, typically in roof framing, so that the frame stays continuous while that column is taken out and replaced during repair.

      It has been in use since at least the Kamakura period (1185 to 1333). In a survey of 104 historical structures in a Kyoto archive, 22 contained it [WA].

      Documented examples include the Mieidō Hall at Senjūji, Mie Prefecture, recorded in its restoration report [SJ], and a full-size specimen at the Takenaka Carpentry Tools Museum, Kobe.

      Variants recorded in the literature: dowels (太枘 dabo), an inserted roof post (koyatsuka), cut from a log, with wedges on the post tenon, with through draw pins (大栓 daisen), and with bolts [WA, Figs 3.2 to 3.7]. Fig. 3.5 shows it on purlins (keta) over a pillar, Fig. 3.2 on roof beams (koyabari).

      The two halves are identical: one is the other turned end over end, so a single cutting programme serves both. Rotate the model to see it.
